Living without water

Minerals do not need an ocean to meet water. On the Moon it takes the form of rims a hundred nanometres thick on a grain. Six stops through the papers I keep coming back to.

Fra Mauro, Apollo 14 · NASA, AS14-68-9487

In November I will be at the 13th Granada–Münster Discussion Meeting in Granada, 25–27 November 2026. This page is not a preview of what I will say there. It is the shelf: the articles I would put in someone’s hands if they asked where to start, in the order I would read them.

Every figure comes from an open-access paper and keeps its licence and credit. Where a paper is not open, I describe it and link it, nothing more.

Follow
1

The Moon · from orbit

Thin, but everywhere

Orbital spectrometers do not see the Moon’s interior, they see a skin: the Moon Mineralogy Mapper is sensitive to roughly the upper millimetre. Li and Milliken mapped hydroxyl and water in that skin and its variation with latitude and time of day; Bandfield and colleagues, working from other spectral data, found OH/H2O at all latitudes and local times, with no significant daily migration, and tied the solar-wind part to defect sites in the minerals.

Read them together: the first gives the map, the second asks whether the water moves with the Sun.

Li, S. & Milliken, R. E. Water on the surface of the Moon as seen by the Moon Mineralogy Mapper: distribution, abundance, and origins. Science Advances 3, e1701471 (2017). Open access, CC BY-NC.

Bandfield, J. L., Poston, M. J., Klima, R. L. & Edwards, C. S. Widespread distribution of OH/H2O on the lunar surface inferred from spectral data. Nature Geoscience 11, 173–177 (2018).

2

The Moon · the grain

Where the hydrogen sits

Down at the scale of a single grain, the story changes. Xu and colleagues measured soil returned by Chang’e-5 from a mid-latitude site and found the topmost ~100 nm of the grains holding between 1,116 and 2,516 ppm of hydrogen, with deuterium values so light they point to the solar wind as the source; in the glass, the profile with depth is bell-shaped, in the minerals it only decreases.

The reason those rims exist at all is older, and it is a classic: Keller and McKay showed in 1997 that most lunar soil grains are wrapped in rims 60–200 nm thick, amorphous or full of nanophase iron, built by solar-wind damage and by vapour from impacts. Pieters and Noble’s review is a good map of what “space weathering” means across airless bodies.

Xu, Y. et al. High abundance of solar wind-derived water in lunar soils from the middle latitude. PNAS (2022). Open access, CC BY-NC-ND.

Keller, L. P. & McKay, D. S. The nature and origin of rims on lunar soil grains. Geochimica et Cosmochimica Acta 61, 2331–2341 (1997).

Pieters, C. M. & Noble, S. K. Space weathering on airless bodies. Journal of Geophysical Research: Planets 121, 1865–1884 (2016).

The Moon · a reservoir

Water in glass beads

He and colleagues looked at impact glass beads from the same Chang’e-5 soil and showed that solar-wind hydrogen does not stay on the outside: it diffuses inward, on timescales they estimate at under 15 years at 360 K, and a thick enough layer of such beads could hold as much as 2.7×1014 kg of water. The schematic below is theirs, and it is a useful picture of how a surface that looks dry can keep a store.

He, H. et al. A solar wind-derived water reservoir on the Moon hosted by impact glass beads. Nature Geoscience 16, 294–300 (2023). doi:10.1038/s41561-023-01159-6

4

The Moon · the cold end

And then, ice

The one place where water does sit as ice is in the permanently shadowed regions. Li and colleagues found spectral features of H2O ice exposed at the optical surface, within 20° of both poles, where annual maximum temperatures stay below 110 K. It is patchy: only about 3.5% of the cold traps show exposed ice, and some pixels may hold around 30 wt% mixed with dry regolith.

Li, S. et al. Direct evidence of surface exposed water ice in the lunar polar regions. PNAS (2018). Open access, CC BY-NC-ND (so no figure here).

5

An asteroid · the opposite problem

Too much water, then none

Bennu is the counterpart to the Moon: water was abundant once, and it left. In samples returned by OSIRIS-REx, McCoy and colleagues found sodium-bearing phosphates, carbonates, sulfates, chlorides and fluorides, the leftovers of a late-stage brine that evaporated early in the history of Bennu’s parent body. They also note that many of these salts decompose with prolonged exposure to Earth’s air, which is why the sample return and its curation matter.

McCoy, T. J. et al. An evaporite sequence from ancient brine recorded in Bennu samples. Nature 637 (2025). doi:10.1038/s41586-024-08495-6

6

Rock on Earth · what water does inside a crystal

The stress is the story

A reaction between a mineral and a little water is not a quiet thing. Plümper and colleagues took marbles containing periclase that had hydrated to brucite and measured what the reaction did to the rock around it: differential stresses of at least 300 MPa, with local maxima around 1.5 GPa, dislocation densities up to 1015 m−2, and a network of reaction-induced cracks that X-ray tomography shows connecting the brucite domains. It is one of those papers where the figure does most of the talking.

The companion question is what water is when it is almost nowhere. Chogani, King, Tutolo, Živković and Plümper’s title says it plainly: the geochemistry of aqueous fluids changes when they are confined in nanometre-scale spaces.

Plümper, O., Wallis, D., Teuling, F., Moulas, E., Schmalholz, S. M., Amiri, H. & Müller, D. High-magnitude stresses induced by mineral-hydration reactions. Geology 50, 1351 (2022). Open access, CC BY.

Chogani, A., King, H. E., Tutolo, B., Živković, A. & Plümper, O. Geochemistry of lithospheric aqueous fluids modified by nanoconfinement. Nature Geoscience 18, 191–196 (2025). doi:10.1038/s41561-024-01629-5
