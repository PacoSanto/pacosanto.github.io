It started with a no

I had an idea for a spin-off. I wrote it up with a colleague and two professors, we sent it to the University of Padova to become a university spin-off, and the answer was no.

I am a geologist, but my days are not all minerals. I spend them reading articles, doing paperwork and following students' theses, and when I manage to find room for research, that means a diffractometer and a handful of microscopes. For a long time I explained my projects the way scientists usually do: a long list of methods, then a long list of results.

After that no, I started reading. Ben Horowitz's The Hard Thing About Hard Things, Peter Thiel's Zero to One, which I read as Da zero a uno, and Eric Ries's The Lean Startup, which I read as Partire leggeri. Since then I keep reading a little economics, and I leaf through Il Sole 24 Ore now and then. None of it was about geology. What it gave me, more than answers, was the habit of asking myself questions, the same ones that funders, industry partners, curators and department heads ask, and they are business-like:

  • Who needs this?
  • What changes if it works?
  • Why you?
  • What does it cost?
  • Who pays?
  • What can go wrong?

Here is one line from each of the three books, next to the part of the canvas where I hear it.

Big initiatives grow out of a no.

That is what I took from it. The no closed one door, and the reading opened a question: how to describe a research project the way the people who decide about it read it.

Why economics can help a geologist

Economics, at its simplest, is the study of choices when resources are scarce. A research project is exactly that: a limited number of months, a limited budget, a limited number of instruments and people, and many things you could do with them. Four ideas from a first economics lesson turn out to be enough.

  • Scarcity and opportunity costEvery month spent on one thing is a month not spent on another. box 10, Expenses
  • Value is for someoneA result is valuable when a particular person can do something they could not do before. box 2, Users · box 3, Outcomes
  • Costs and incomeWhat the project spends, and who pays for it. box 10 · box 11, Funding
  • RiskWhat can go wrong, and what you do about it. box 12, Risks

What is a canvas?

A canvas is a single page divided into boxes, each one answering one question, so that a whole idea can be seen, discussed and criticised at once.

The best known is the Business Model Canvas by Alexander Osterwalder and Yves Pigneur, which fits a company on a page: who the customers are, what value you offer them, what it costs, where the money comes from. The Lean Canvas, adapted from it by Ash Maurya, does the same for a start-up that is still testing its idea. Mine is an adaptation of the Research Canvas by Dr Katia Smith-Litière, made for the Postdocs to Innovators (p2i) Network on the same logic, with customers renamed users and revenue renamed funding. Press In business terms on the filled example below to see each box next to its business counterpart.

Three lenses and a pitch

The thirteen boxes come in three colours. They are three lenses borrowed from design thinking, and a good project needs all of them.

Unique Value Proposition

In business terms: Unique value proposition

Lime mortars protect our heritage and emit less CO2 than cement, but water and dirt wear them out.

We teach lime to repel water and keep itself clean, the way a lotus leaf does.

Longer-lasting, low-carbon mortars and gentler treatments for historic buildings.

How I built it

I built it in LaTeX with TikZ, for three reasons. It can be versioned on GitHub like code. It prints cleanly on an A4 landscape page, or on a wall. And it can sit on its own or as the last page of a proposal. Each box is a single call, so moving or resizing one means changing a few numbers:

\rcbox{x}{y}{width}{height}{colour}{number}{title}{content}

With a LaTeX distribution, make builds the empty template and make example builds the filled one. Without one, upload the folder to Overleaf and compile. The repository also has a mode for dropping the canvas at the end of a longer document.

What I like most is a constraint. The boxes have a fixed size, so text that does not fit is text that is not clear yet: I cut words, I never shrink the font. It is also the first rule of five, in the order I would give them to a colleague:

  1. Keywords, not sentences: one or two lines per point.
  2. Respect the box: text that does not fit is not clear yet.
  3. Write n/a in a box that does not apply, never leave it empty.
  4. Be concrete: numbers, names, dates, calls. No adjectives.
  5. Read box 13 aloud to someone outside your field.

Start from the problem and end with the pitch. If you cannot write box 13 in plain words after filling the others, the project is not clear yet, and it is better to find out now than in front of a panel. I filled my own example retrospectively, and two boxes still carry a tbc: expenses and funding. They are the money boxes, the ones I used to skip.

Use it, break it, translate it

The canvas is free to use, share and adapt, also commercially, under CC BY-SA 3.0: give credit and share your version under the same licence. The repository has the empty template, the filled example, a Makefile, and works on Overleaf.

Open the repository on GitHub

If you use it, I would love to see it. Open an issue or a pull request, especially for:
