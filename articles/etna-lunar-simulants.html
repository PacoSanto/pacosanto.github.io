Volcanic deposits from Mount Etna (Italy) as high-fidelity lunar simulants for In-Situ Resource Utilization (ISRU) applications

To build on the Moon you first have to practise on Earth, with something that behaves like lunar soil. We found a close match on the slopes of Etna: a volcanic deposit that resembles what Apollo 14 brought home, and that can be turned into building material and oxygen.

G. Melchiori, F. Santoro De Vico (corresponding author), A. Dottori, R. Pozzobon, L. Valentini, P. Ferretti, A. Bonetto, M. Lavagna, S. Calvari, M. Massironi

380 kg of Moon

That is about how much soil and rock the Apollo missions brought back. It is far too little, and far too precious, to test a construction method or an oxygen reactor on. So engineers work with lunar simulants: terrestrial materials that stand in for the real regolith.

Most simulants are produced in the United States, only a few come from Europe, and commercial availability remains limited. European volcanic provinces have been largely unexplored for this purpose.

Etna is an unusual place to start. At the meeting point of the African and Eurasian plates, where the Ionian lithosphere subducts beneath the Calabrian Arc, it has spent about 500,000 years producing varied volcanic deposits, with compositions spanning both mare-like and highland-like lithologies. And Sicilian law already allows volcanic ash fallout to be reused in place of raw materials.

Is there a natural deposit on Etna close enough to real lunar soil to work as a high-fidelity simulant, and can it actually be turned into building material and oxygen?

Three sites, six samples

  • 2,600 m

    Cisternazza pit crater

    Mantled by fresh pyroclastic fall deposits, 2–64 mm, from the paroxysmal events of the last four decades. Unweathered material.

    CL2 · CM1 · CM3
  • 1,790 m

    Monte Nunziata lava tube

    Pyroclastic deposits behind the lining wall of the 1843 tube, potentially similar to material in lunar lava tube environments.

    MN1
  • 1,625 m

    Tre Livelli lava tube system

    Samples from different levels of the 1792–93 tube, to capture the variability inside one volcanic structure.

    TL4 · TL5

Where it all comes from

We started from a modified version of the NASA workflow for simulant characterisation. Because the goal was ISRU, we put chemistry first: even a chemically poor simulant can serve geotechnical tests if its grain size is right.

The twin test

The reference is sample 14259, a regolith collected 125 m west of the Apollo 14 Lunar Module. Scroll to set the Etna candidates against it, measurement after measurement. All values come from the paper.

14259 · Apollo 14

Test 1 · Chemistry

The screening

A principal component analysis of X-ray fluorescence data set six Etna samples against Apollo compositions and established simulants. Two of them clustered with Apollo 14. The first is CL2, pyroclastics from Cisternazza.

The big oxides line up. The alkalis do not, and we come back to that below.

Test 1 · Chemistry

A second candidate

TL5, from the Tre Livelli lava tube, looks just as promising. On chemistry alone there would be no reason to choose between them.

Test 2 · Mineralogy

One goes out

X-ray diffraction with Rietveld refinement separates them at once. TL5 has too much feldspar and no glass at all. Lunar regolith is full of glass, from impact melting and explosive volcanism, so TL5 is disqualified.

Test 2 · Mineralogy

The right cocktail

CL2 carries plagioclase, pyroxene and olivine, plus about 50 % glass against 47.7 % in the Moon sample. Its glass is even the right kind: by the MgO/Al₂O₃ and CaO/Al₂O₃ ratios used for lunar glasses (0.29 and 0.55), it plots in the highland impact-glass field.

Test 3 · Grain size

Sieved to match

Grain size controls how a soil behaves mechanically. We crushed CL2 and rebuilt its distribution through four sieves to align it with Apollo samples, and the curve follows 14259 closely: Pearson r = 0.84, with a low Jensen-Shannon divergence (0.083).

Test 4 · Light

The spectral fingerprint

In the 0.4–2.5 µm range, the 1-µm absorption band of CL2 aligns best with the agglutinate fraction of 14259: clusters of mineral fragments welded by impact glass, which can make up 60–70 % of some lunar soils.

The centres sit a few tens of nanometres apart, because Etna's pyroxene is high-Ca augite and the agglutinates are dominated by low-Ca orthopyroxene.

What it means for instruments

Space weathering makes lunar spectra darker and redder, and it cannot be fully replicated on Earth. Even so, the match around 1 µm points to a further use: testing the optical instruments and remote-sensing technologies designed for lunar exploration.

From powder to bricks

Passing as Moon dust is one thing, working like building material is another. We mixed CL2 powder with alkaline activators into four formulations, cast them as 40 × 40 × 160 mm prisms, and cured them for 28 days at 25 °C and 65 % relative humidity.

The strongest mix is also the least predictable. For robots building on the Moon that matters: adding 3 % urea gives up 11.6 % of the mean strength but brings the coefficient of variation down from 20.5 % to 6.9 %.

Oxygen from rock

The same powder went into a carbothermal reduction plant: 50 g sieved to 250 µm, at 1,100 °C for 8 hours, alternating an 85/15 H₂/CH₄ mixture with pure hydrogen. Oxygen comes out as carbon oxides, later converted to water.

CL2 behaved like the certified highland simulants NU-LHT-2M and LHS-1. Its higher ferrous oxide content made it more reactive with hydrogen, supporting faster reduction during the hydrogen-only steps. Less water than expected was actually recovered, which we attribute to the low efficiency of the plant's condensation stage.

Not a perfect twin

Too many alkalis
Na₂O 3.3 % against 0.7 %, K₂O 1.8 % against 0.5 %. Expected: the Moon's stock of volatile lithophile elements is lower than Earth's primitive upper mantle by a factor of 4–5.
A different origin for the glass
Etna's formed in rapid cooling during explosive eruptions, the Moon's under meteorite bombardment. Similar amount and composition, different history. In our tests composition seemed to matter more.
Earth is not the Moon
All of this ran at 1 atm. Thermal cycling (about 30 %) plus vacuum effects (up to about 40 %) could add up to a 60–70 % loss of strength, close to structural limits. Thermally stable sites or regolith cover could help.
But gravity helps
Structures bear one sixth of the load they would on Earth. A 16 MPa material degraded to 10 MPa would still perform like 60 MPa here, if the combined damage stays below that.

What comes next

The conclusion is specific: CL2 from Cisternazza is a high-fidelity simulant for Apollo 14 Fra Mauro materials, with strong potential for lunar construction and resource extraction. A "perfect" simulant remains out of reach, since no terrestrial rock has lived through space weathering.

The next step is to test both processes in a vacuum chamber, to measure how much of this survives real lunar conditions. And because the material is abundant and easy to reach, the simulant can be refined as ISRU technologies evolve.
